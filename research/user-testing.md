# Methodological options for evaluating the Kratt wake-word prototype

## Bottom line

For a **10-minute** prototype test with **20–30 participants**, the most defensible design is a **hybrid** one: keep the **objective telemetry** as the primary evidence for wake-word behavior, add **one established short subjective measure** that fits task-based usability testing, and retain your current custom items only as **researcher-developed diagnostic items**, not as a validated scale. That approach is methodologically consistent with the classic usability split into **effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction**, with spoken-dialogue evaluation traditions such as **PARADISE**, and with later usability-method papers arguing that usability should be assessed with **distinct, justified measures rather than a single ad hoc summary score**. citeturn39view0turn24view1turn30view1turn12view0

The critical finding is this: your current 4+1 questionnaire is **not** indefensible in itself, but it becomes hard to defend if it is presented as a **validated instrument**, if you compute a **single total score** from it, or if you use it as the **only subjective evidence** for “usability” or “acceptance.” Standardized questionnaires generally perform better than home-grown ones in typical small usability-test samples, and prior work explicitly raised reliability concerns about custom questionnaires in such settings. citeturn37view0turn30view1turn6view2

My strongest recommendation is therefore: **do not replace the objective metrics**, **do add a standardized short measure**, and **reframe the current custom items as supplementary descriptive items mapped to established constructs**. If the defense committee insists on seeing a named validated scale, add one. If they care most about local-language practicality, the clearest documented Estonian option I found is **UEQ/UEQ-S**. If they care most about international recognizability, **SUS** remains the best-known benchmark, though I did **not** locate a dedicated psychometrically validated Estonian SUS paper in the accessible sources. citeturn7view0turn26view2turn19search3turn21view1

## Why objective metrics should remain primary

Your main scientific questions are about **model behavior with real users**: positive wake-word recall, hard-negative/confusable false positives, end-to-end task success, and latency. Those are not secondary “UX impressions”; they are direct measures of whether the system works. That aligns closely with ISO 9241-11, which frames usability around outcomes of use, and with wake-word / keyword-spotting research, where false accepts, false rejects, and latency are central performance criteria because false accepts are annoying and privacy-relevant, while misses and delay directly damage the interaction. citeturn39view0turn34view0turn32search2turn35search2

That means the questionnaire should be positioned as **secondary evidence** that helps interpret the logged data, not as the main proof of success. In spoken-dialogue-system evaluation, the classic PARADISE framework is influential precisely because it combines **task success**, **dialogue costs**, and **user satisfaction** rather than relying on one questionnaire alone. In other words, your study design is already moving in the right direction: objective metrics first, subjective ratings second. citeturn24view1turn0search11

For this specific prototype, I would map the primary outcomes as follows. **Positive wake-word recall** and **hard-negative false positives** speak to effectiveness and reliability. **End-to-end lamp-control success** is the most concrete task-effectiveness measure. **Latency** is the key efficiency measure. The subjective questionnaire should then focus narrowly on perceived reliability, perceived speed, naturalness of phrasing, and willingness to use, without pretending to replace the telemetry. That division of labor is much easier to defend than trying to make a brief custom scale carry the full burden of “usability measurement.” citeturn39view0turn24view1turn21view1

A further methodological advantage is that task-based post-task ratings can be asked **immediately after a task**, which usability researchers have argued improves diagnostic value and validity relative to vague overall impressions. For a very short study, that is exactly what you want: one or two focused ratings placed right after the wake-word or bulb-control tasks, not a large post-hoc battery. citeturn12view0turn11search15

## Estonian-language practicality

The clearest positive finding for the **Estonian-language** context is **UEQ/UEQ-S**. The official UEQ materials list an Estonian version, and a 2018 paper on the Estonian adaptation reported that the translation process followed the UEQ team’s guidance and that the resulting version showed **satisfactory reliability overall**, although one subscale was weaker. For a thesis committee worried about language validity, that is the cleanest translation/availability argument I found. citeturn7view0turn6view0turn26view2

For **NASA-TLX**, I found an Estonian worksheet on a local ergonomics site and the official NASA materials, which suggests practical availability, but I did **not** find a dedicated Estonian psychometric validation paper in the accessible sources. So NASA-TLX is practically translatable in Estonian, but not the strongest choice unless workload is central to the study. citeturn26view0turn26view1

For **SUS**, **UMUX-LITE**, **SEQ**, **ASQ**, **SASSI**, and **VUS**, I did **not** locate accessible dedicated Estonian validation papers. That does not mean you cannot translate them carefully, but it does mean you should avoid claiming that you used an already “validated Estonian version” unless you can document it independently. For SUS specifically, this matters because prior work with non-native English speakers found comprehension problems with the English item containing the word “cumbersome,” so simply administering the English form to Estonian-speaking participants for convenience would be a poor choice. citeturn6view2turn5search1

Methodologically, this pushes the options in two different directions. If you prioritize **official Estonian availability**, **UEQ-S** is the strongest documented short instrument I found. If you prioritize **task-based fit and minimal burden**, **SEQ** is stronger conceptually, even though I did not find an Estonian validation paper for it. That is why the final recommendation below is a hybrid rather than a single-scale replacement strategy. citeturn26view2turn12view0

## Ranked recommendation

**First choice: keep the objective metrics, add SEQ, and keep the current custom items only as mapped diagnostic items.** This is the best fit to your actual research question. SEQ is established, extremely short, and designed for post-task usability testing; it therefore complements your end-to-end bulb-control task well. Your current items on reliability, speed, natural command phrasing, and future use can then remain as **descriptive secondary evidence**, reported item-by-item and clearly mapped to constructs, without overstating their psychometric status. This option preserves low participant burden while removing the most serious methodological criticism. citeturn12view0turn11search15turn30view1turn37view0

**Second choice: if the committee wants a named benchmark scale, add SUS and keep one or two targeted wake-word items.** SUS is still the most recognizable usability questionnaire, it remains common in voice-interface studies, and recent evidence supports its use for voice user interfaces. The downside is that I did not find a dedicated validated Estonian SUS, so you would need a careful translation process and honest wording in the thesis about that limitation. In return, you get a benchmark likely to be familiar to examiners. citeturn21view1turn19search3turn37view0

**Third choice: if Estonian-language defensibility matters more than benchmark familiarity, use UEQ-S instead of SUS.** The main reason to choose UEQ-S is not superior construct fit; it is the presence of a documented Estonian version and a short 8-item format. This is the safest “validated Estonian short scale” path I found. The tradeoff is that UEQ-S measures broader UX, including hedonic aspects that are not central to wake-word model behavior. citeturn7view0turn6view0turn26view2

**Fourth choice: use UMUX-LITE only if every extra item matters.** UMUX-LITE is attractive because it is only two items and later comparisons found it to track SUS closely. Still, it is very narrow, not voice-specific, and the original 2013 paper itself was relatively cautious about using it as a standalone replacement until more validation accumulated. In a thesis defense, that makes it a good ultra-short option, but not the strongest flagship instrument if you can afford a few more items. citeturn14view0turn38search2

**Fifth choice: do not use SASSI, VUS, SUISQ, TAM/UTAUT, or NASA-TLX as the main questionnaire for this study.** SASSI and VUS are more voice-specific, but they are too long for your study and have no documented Estonian availability in the sources I found. TAM/UTAUT are useful as theoretical background for “would use at home,” but they are acceptance models, not short usability-test questionnaires. NASA-TLX only makes sense if workload becomes an explicit research variable rather than an incidental concern. citeturn40view0turn15view1turn28search0turn28search4turn26view0

## Thesis-ready justification

The final methodological argument I would use is this: the study’s **primary outcomes** are objective and system-centered, because the thesis is fundamentally about real-user behavior of a wake-word model, not about benchmarking a mature consumer product. The objective measures capture effectiveness and efficiency directly: recall of “Kuule Kratt,” hard-negative false activations on confusable phrases, task success in the lamp-control interaction, and latency. A short standardized rating such as SEQ then adds a validated perception measure for the user-facing task experience, while the custom items provide diagnostic detail on reliability, speed, naturalness, and domestic-use willingness. This is acceptable because the custom items are not treated as a validated scale and are not used as the sole source of evidence. citeturn39view0turn34view0turn12view0turn24view1turn30view1

The custom items can be defended if they are mapped explicitly to established constructs:

| Current item | Construct it can legitimately support | How to treat it |
|---|---|---|
| “The system reacted reliably enough.” | Perceived reliability / response accuracy / effectiveness | Descriptive, item-level |
| “The system reacted quickly enough.” | Perceived speed / latency / efficiency | Descriptive, item-level |
| “Command phrasing felt natural.” | Naturalness / habitability / semantic appropriateness | Descriptive, item-level |
| “I would use such a system at home.” | Behavioral intention / acceptance | Descriptive only, not a usability score |
| Open annoyance/surprise question | Qualitative incident capture | Qualitative coding or thematic summary |

What you should **not** do is combine those items into a single homemade “usability score,” calculate Cronbach’s alpha and claim scale validation, or imply that these heterogeneous items all measure one latent construct. Method papers on usability measurement explicitly warn against poor aggregation and emphasize distinguishing usability aspects. citeturn30view1turn37view0

The main alternatives and the reasons for rejecting or adapting them are straightforward. **SASSI** was considered because it is speech-focused and includes habitability and speed, which are relevant to voice interaction, but it is far too long for a 10-minute study and its original development history is more tentative than many people assume. **VUS** was considered because it was designed for voice assistants specifically, but it is still too long and lacks confirmed Estonian availability. **PARADISE** is conceptually attractive because it mirrors your objective + subjective mixed design, but it is a framework, not a drop-in short questionnaire. **SUS** remains a strong fallback benchmark, but its Estonian-language status is weaker in the accessible sources than UEQ-S. **NASA-TLX** and **TAM/UTAUT** are only justified if workload or adoption becomes a primary question, which is not your current thesis focus. citeturn40view0turn40view1turn15view1turn24view1turn19search3turn26view2turn26view0turn28search0turn28search4

The honest limitations to state are also clear. The study is short, exposure is brief, and the prototype controls only one bulb through one scripted smart-home pathway, so the results cannot be generalized to long-term household adoption. The sample size is sufficient for a small prototype usability study and for descriptive questionnaire summaries, but not for validating a new psychometric scale of your own. If you use a translated SUS, SEQ, or ASQ in Estonian, you should say plainly that you used a careful study translation rather than a previously validated Estonian version, unless you later verify one independently. And if you keep the “would use at home” item, you should describe it as a **single-item adoption indicator**, not as a full technology-acceptance measurement. citeturn37view0turn6view2turn5search1turn28search0turn28search4
