## 2. Abstract (in English)

This bachelor's thesis investigates how to build and evaluate an Estonian wake-word detector for a resource-constrained smart-home microcontroller. The work focuses on the "Kratt" project, whose target is local detection of the phrase "Kuule Kratt" on an ESP32-S3 class device without relying on cloud services. The practical motivation is the voice-assistant support gap for small languages: while Estonian speech-to-text already has viable local models, local wake-word detection is the front-end component that determines the usability of the entire voice pipeline, and Estonian is not covered by the distributed models of the open `openWakeWord` framework or by the closed-source Picovoice Porcupine reference.

The methodological core of the thesis is the reconstruction and refinement of a `microWakeWord`-based training and evaluation pipeline implemented on top of TensorFlow, with TFLite export to an ESP32-S3 device integrated through ESPHome and the Home Assistant `voice_assistant` interface. Before moving to Estonian data, the pipeline was validated with a public control experiment on the `Speech Commands` dataset, targeting the keyword `marvin`. This control experiment exposed a data-leakage risk in the original clip-level evaluation: the held-out evaluation set partially overlapped with the training set, producing misleadingly optimistic clip-level false-positive rates that did not predict streaming behaviour on a MacBook Pro microphone, where the same model produced approximately 50 false activations per hour. To mitigate this risk, the work introduces independent held-out test sets, explicit train–test disjointness checks, and adopts streaming evaluation with FAPH (false activations per hour) as the central false-trigger metric. Confidence intervals are reported throughout: Wilson score intervals for clip-level proportions and exact Poisson–Garwood intervals (with the rule-of-three upper bound for zero-event observations) for FAPH, and four explicitly distinguished FAPH variants (framework, scripted offline replay, field, user-study replay) are tracked to keep counting rules reproducible.

The refined evaluation shows that wake-word model quality cannot be characterised by recall or clip-level false-positive rate alone: the strongest models differ across metrics and across data sources. The thesis documents this through three successive validation rounds. The first round revealed train–test contamination behind apparently strong clip-level scores. The second round revealed second-order labelling defects in the positive class, where some short or automatically generated clips did not in fact carry the full target phrase, leading models to fire on the prefix "kuule" or the colloquial form "kule" alone; this motivated dedicated phrase-structure tests for prefix, single-word, swapped-order, and "kuule"/"kule" confusables. The third round showed that selecting checkpoints solely to minimise ambient FAPH produces models that are silent against all unseen signals, including real target speakers, so the checkpoint selection criterion must be composite. In diagnostic experiments, consensus among specialised expert models substantially reduced ambient-speech FAPH and achieved FAPH = 0.79 on a held-out Common Voice Estonian speech track, but at the cost of recall, and no single model or combination simultaneously satisfied recall ≥ 0.95 and FAPH < 1.

The main contribution of the thesis is to show that, in small-language local wake-word development, the central challenge is not only model training but also methodologically sound evaluation and evidence discipline. The deliverables are a reproducible pipeline, a documented data and evaluation protocol, and an empirical mapping of the trade-offs between recall, target-similar negative rejection, prefix and confusable false positives, and false activations for the Estonian wake phrase "Kuule Kratt". A scenario-based evaluation flow with annotated long-form ambient recordings is proposed as a transferable protocol for other low-resource languages. End-to-end deployability is demonstrated through ESPHome and the Home Assistant `voice_assistant` interface with candidate model `v16c`; the final deployment decision is deferred to an ongoing user study with 20–30 participants, whose recordings are replayed across multiple shadow models at frozen thresholds. The thesis also reflects on the role of agentic software development as a tool amplifier that shifts the bottleneck from implementation effort to the harder problem of acquiring real-speaker evidence.
